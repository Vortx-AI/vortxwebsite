We've spent the last couple of years on a fairly specific problem: AI agents running inside large companies are routinely answering questions about specific places without any real information on those places, and we don't think that gets solved by training a bigger model. It gets solved by giving the model a memory of the real world that it can actually cite. emem.dev is the open version of that memory, free and on GitHub. geo.qa is the enterprise version, where customers form a private one over their own sensors and operations.

Why we built this

If you've worked with an AI agent in production, you've probably noticed it will confidently answer questions about specific physical places it has no real information on. Has this address flooded recently. What's the air quality at this stadium right now. Did this farm get rain last week. The agent picks something up from a static dataset, paraphrases it, serves it as live truth. Sometimes that's fine. A lot of the time it isn't, and there's no way for the person on the other end to tell which is which.

Our take, after some time spent in the weeds with operators, is that this isn't really a model problem. It's a memory problem. The agent doesn't have a place to look things up that it can trust, and it can't show its work even if it wanted to. So we built that place. The unromantic engineering version is this: every place on Earth gets a stable address, every observation about that place becomes a fact at the address, and every fact carries a cryptographic receipt you can verify without us being in the loop.

Architecturally there's one system underneath. Product-wise there are two. Encoders sit at the source, wherever a useful lens already does, from orbit down to a fixed camera in a warehouse, and turn what they see into compact semantic representations that travel cheap. Two decoders sit on the read side. emem.dev is the open one; anyone can run a node, anyone can read. geo.qa is the private one, where a customer's own modalities form a memory only their tenancy can see. Most teams use both.

Encoders, basically anywhere there's a lens

We started in orbit because that's where the original satellite-imagery work lived, but the encoder doesn't really care where the lens is. It runs today on Sentinel-2 tiles, drone passes over pipeline corridors, vehicle dashcam feeds, fixed cameras inside distribution centres. Whatever a lens sees, the encoder turns into a compact semantic vector that's cheap to move and small enough to actually transmit from a satellite without dragging the imagery through a ground station.

The choice between the two products is mostly about who owns the memory

emem.dev is for the open layer of Earth memory anyone should be able to read and verify, the way you'd query OpenStreetMap or pull a Sentinel granule. geo.qa is for the layer that shouldn't be public, because it's an operator's own cameras, drones, files, and sensors. We made them speak the same protocol on purpose, so an agent can stitch a public fact and a private one together in a single answer without caring which is which.

The receipts are the part that actually matters

Every fact in the system carries an ed25519 signature and a blake3 content hash. That sounds like crypto theatre at first; in practice it has a very mundane effect, which is that an agent quoting a fact is also quoting a receipt anyone can re-pull and verify without us being in the loop. That's the property that turns Earth memory from a closed product into something an agent can actually cite when a person on the other end pushes back.

[ Architecture ]

A single algebra: Cell × Band × Tslot → Fact

The boring version is that every spot on Earth gets a stable address called a cell64 (about ten metres on a side), and any observation about that spot lives at the address as a fact, with metadata that says what band it's in, what time it covers, what value it has, and what signed receipt it was issued under. Both emem.dev and your geo.qa tenancy use the same call shape to read this. So the boundary between public and private is just a question of where the data sits, not a different protocol. An agent can pull elevation from emem.dev, pull yesterday's drone overflight from your private memory, and cite both in one answer.

Where this actually shows up

These are the surfaces we keep ending up on with operators. Honestly the line between "this thing is feeding the encoder" and "this thing is reading the decoder" is fuzzier than the diagram suggests; most of these are doing both at once.

Autonomous vehicles

A modern AV is already carrying somewhere between six and ten cameras and a couple of LIDAR units, all of which can be writing to the memory while the car is also reading from neighbouring cells to know what's around the corner before seeing it. The cite-the-receipt part matters here mostly because a decision that fails an audit later needs to be able to point at what the car knew, and when.

Robotics

The interesting case in robotics is the fleet, not the individual machine. One delivery bot or warehouse arm doesn't need much memory; a fleet that's writing what it sees and reading what its peers wrote five minutes ago effectively shares a single understanding of the building it operates in, and that understanding gets richer every shift.

UAVs & drones

This is where most of our early enterprise traffic comes from. Operators flying repeat passes over a pipeline corridor or a farm grid have a real change-detection problem to solve, and "did anything change in sector seven this week" turns out to be exactly the kind of question that goes from impossible to trivial once each overflight is writing signed cells the next agent can re-read.

Aviation

For approach planning and corridor awareness, especially in GPS-denied or low-visibility conditions, an aircraft can read the terrain memory the same way it reads a METAR. We're not pretending this replaces certified avionics; it's a layer underneath, the way a moving map is.

Defense & security

The pattern here is persistent change detection over installations and perimeters, where the private tenancy of geo.qa is non-negotiable because classified context can't leave the boundary. emem.dev's public cells fill in the open-source layer of the world underneath, which is sometimes useful and sometimes ignored, depending on the customer.

Mobile & edge

A phone with an MCP client can do most of what we'd want a small device to do here, both as an encoder source for whatever it's pointed at and as a reader of the surrounding memory. We're keeping a close eye on what happens when this gets distilled into something a couple of orders of magnitude smaller, but for now we mostly let teams build their own edge clients.

Industrial & factories

On the floor, fixed cameras can feed the encoder while production-side agents read back the decoder before they make a call. The honest thing to say is that this overlaps with what a good MES already does, and we're most useful when the operator wants a memory layer that lives across multiple sites instead of inside one.

Agriculture & environment

The public side of emem.dev covers most of what an environmental analyst would want already (crop stress, evapotranspiration, deforestation, mangrove change, soil moisture proxies), so the interesting move for an actual ag operator is layering their own ground truth on top through geo.qa, and letting agents reason across both at once.

The two products in detail

The split is mostly about ownership and who gets to read. Both products serve agents and humans equally; the audience question we used to lead with turned out to be the wrong frame. Most of our enterprise customers run both: the public layer underneath, their private one on top.

Public · Apache-2.0

emem.dev

The open Earth memory protocol.

emem.dev is what we wish had existed when we started: a public, signed, content-addressed layer of Earth memory that any agent can read without negotiating an API contract first. The whole thing is Apache-2.0 on GitHub. Run a node yourself if you want; the receipts are still verifiable when we're not in the loop.

  • No keys, no accounts. The read endpoints (/v1/locate, /v1/recall, /v1/diff) take plain HTTP. There's also an MCP server at emem.dev/mcp that any modern agent client picks up automatically.
  • Verifiable offline. Recompute blake3 over the canonical CBOR, verify the ed25519 signature against the responder's public key, you're done. No round-trip required to trust an answer someone hands you.
  • 34 bands across the open-data subset. Sentinel-1/2, MODIS, Copernicus DEM, JRC water, Hansen forest, plus foundation embeddings from Tessera, Prithvi, and Galileo. About 1792 dimensions per cell once everything's stacked.

Private · Your tenancy

geo.qa

An Earth memory that's yours, on your data.

geo.qa is what we sell to enterprise customers whose AI has to actually be right about specific places and times: pipeline operators, airports, insurance carriers, utilities, defense. The same protocol underneath, but now the cells are fed by your cameras, your drones, your satellites, your files, your sensors, and the whole thing lives inside a tenancy only your agents can read. emem.dev is pre-wired so the public layer just works.

  • Twelve modalities, one fused memory. Cameras, drones, satellites, sensors, files, OSM, MCP, plus the rest. We register CCTV by lat/lon/heading/pitch/FOV, run SAM3 segmentation on demand, and fuse everything into 1024-dimensional vectors.
  • Where it lives. Persisted in PostgreSQL with a Vespa 1024-D vector index for retrieval, scoped to your tenancy. Six ingest pipelines, one destination. The vector breakdown if you care: visual 512, semantic 384, spatial 128, geo 128, temporal 64, overlay 64.
  • The tenancy boundary is real. Per-org filesystem scoping plus a query-layer organisation filter on every read. Nothing your team writes ever leaks outward into the public emem.dev registry. The boundary is enforced in the codebase, not in marketing copy.
